You're right that from a pure RESTful perspective, DELETE should mean delete—gone, vanished, 404 on the next GET. That's the textbook definition, and if the system lived in isolation, I'd agree with you 100%.

The reality: in distributed systems, especially ones that deal with financial records, invoices, audit trails, and event-driven architecture (Kafka, logs, downstream systems), data has long tails. A product "deleted" in one place may still need to exist conceptually in another for the sake of consistency, or regulatory compliance.

You said, "Users don’t care about your internal kitchen."

True — users don’t care about how the sausage is made. But they do care when the sausage mysteriously disappears from their order history.

If I hard-delete a product and a user pulls up their invoice history and sees “Product not found” or blank lines where product names should be, that’s a broken experience. Technically correct, but functionally flawed. Users want coherence, not dogma.

What I'm doing isn’t leaking internal mechanics — it's about preserving system truth in a user-facing way. I’m saying, "Hey, this product isn’t active anymore, but here’s its last known state.” That’s not a technical detail — that’s a business reality.

Use PATCH/PUT/POST for status updates instead of DELETE - But users expect a DELETE endpoint to be there. By handling it with a soft-delete and messaging clearly in the response ("Product moved to inactive status"), we don’t violate expectations — we clarify them.

In the end, REST is a guideline, not stric rules And in real-world systems, clarity and robustness often matter more than guideline.
