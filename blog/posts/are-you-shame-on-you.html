Are you shame on you?

The reality of society, social media and movie actors. The Ideal Body Trap

Shame was recognized as an emotion in ancient times. In 2025, there is evidence of how shame works and how body shaming affects a person.

  1. Does body shaming give someone motivation?
  2. Does anything good happen because of it?
  3. Where did this shame develop and evolve?
  4. How does the internet make it worse?
  5. How does it affect a person’s mental health?

Lets go through these questions one by one.

The basic Human emotions are

  1. Anger
  2. Fear
  3. Sadness
  4. Happiness

These are emotions we share with most animals.

Some emotions like

  1. Shame
  2. embarrassment
  3. pride
  4. guilt

are considered secondary emotions.

We don’t share these with other animals.

Some developmental psychologists say these secondary emotions start unfolding within the first few months after birth. Others say, they develop later, after around two years.

As we grow, we start understanding how others see us.

  1. what they think about us?
  2. whether they accept us?
  3. what they don’t like about us?

We understand these as rules of society and start behaving accordingly.

During that time, harsh moments or incidents we experience, consciously or subconsciously they get stored in memory. When similar situations happen again, the emotion of shame emerges from our body.

But this is also a survival emotion. It acts as an indicator to maintain belonging and prevent rejection.

We want to be liked by others, at least by people we like.

“Shame is an emotion we don’t like and want to avoid, because it relates to being seen as inferior, looked down upon, or seen as embarrassing. “ Shame (1998)

When we are body shamed, our brain processes it as being socially inferior.

This is often supported in movies. For example,

  • if someone is bald, they are used for one-line jokes.
  • If someone is overweight, they are also used for jokes or given roles like comedian, side character, or someone to be made fun of.
Media creates stereotypes about how such people should be judged.

Male models in ads are usually lean, muscular, light-skinned or slightly tanned, and tall. This creates a standard of an “ideal body.”

Boys grow up seeing this and start believing that only if they look like that, they will be respected. 
When they grow up and don’t match that image, they develop body dissatisfaction.

In the fitness and supplement industry, top athletes were used for promotion.

They were used repeatedly in ads with taglines like “big, bold, beautiful,” indirectly suggesting that this is the type of body women want.

Movies also reinforce this. In superhero movies like Captain America, the character starts skinny and then transforms into a muscular body. After that, he gets attention and admiration.

This creates aspiration.

most people won’t reach that standard. When we internalize these cultural standards, men especially become dissatisfied with their current body.

Shame, unlike guilt, usually does not push us into action.

Guilt can make us fix something. Shame, in most cases, does not.

Instead, it makes us withdraw:

  1. We don’t want to go outside
  2. We don’t want to show our face
  3. We want to hide

When someone feels body shame, they usually don’t act and transform because of it. Instead, they hide. That’s why many people who go through body transformation are not visible on social media until they achieve results. They come out only after transforming.

Common body shaming includes:

  • Being short
  • Being skinny
  • Being overweight
  • Not matching the “ideal” look

Almost everyone has faced this at some point, even if they don’t talk about it.

One very normalized form is baldness. It is often treated as a joke. But it affects people the same way as other forms of body shaming.

Male pattern baldness (androgenetic alopecia) is genetic. If it exists in your family line or even on the mother’s side, it can be passed down.

Men who experience hair loss often have lower confidence and lower quality of life because they keep thinking about it.

Society also mocks them. calling them “uncle” which increases fear.

Fears like

  • What if no one finds me attractive?
  • What if I don’t get married?
  • What if people judge me?

Even slight hair loss or slight weight gain can trigger this fear.

To fit into society, people start actively shaping their personality to avoid rejection.

Social Identity Theory

People define themselves based on groups.

They see how people with similar traits are treated in media, among friends, in family and internalize that.

“If people like me are treated like this, then this is my value.”

If someone lacks height, muscles, or certain looks, it can feel like a threat to masculinity.

In psychology, this is called a masculinity threat response.

The same applies to fat shaming. There is also something called fat phobia which is an extreme fear or aversion toward fatness.

Fat Phobia

Many people develop this through media, environment, and social conditioning.

Even someone who was once overweight may develop hatred toward fat.

This insecurity can get projected outward. judging or mocking others for small differences.

People who consume a lot of fitness or transformation content may develop more insecurity, especially if they cannot achieve those results.

Such content often,

  • Villainizes fat
  • Glorifies a specific body type
  • Uses fear-based messaging

Some fitness creators use lines

  1. “If you have belly fat, fix it now.”
  2. “If you are too skinny, gain weight fast.”

They create fear first, then give solutions. This can feel condescending, when they already have the “ideal” body.

This triggers shame. Again, it does not make most people act. It makes them hide.

Height has the same issue. On dating or matrimonial sites, there are height filters. If you don’t meet the minimum, you don’t even appear in the options.

That itself creates insecurity.

All this can lead to body dysmorphia

Body Dysmorphia

A mental health condition where a person keeps worrying about small or invisible flaws in their appearance.

Even if others don’t notice, the person keeps thinking about it again and again.

BMI was developed in the 1800s using limited data. It does not consider muscle mass or many important factors. Still, many doctors rely on it and give generic advice.

This can lead to poor healthcare. Real problems may be ignored, and people are told to lose weight first. Because of this, some people feel ashamed and avoid going to doctors altogether.

There is a pressure from everywhere

  • society
  • doctors
  • influencers.
  • peers

Because of this pressure, people rush into extreme diets.

Fad diets promise quick results like losing several kilos in a short time. But the success rate is low, around 10 to 20%.

Most of the weight lost is water. Once normal eating resumes, the weight returns.

This creates a bad relationship with food.

People start fearing food. Even something like Burger becomes scary. They avoid it completely, thinking it will ruin everything.

They keep checking:

  • “Did I lose weight today?”
  • “Is my body improving?”

Taking photos daily, checking the mirror, touching their body repeatedly — this becomes a cycle.

If results don’t show, they feel upset, like everything is going wrong. All this mental load drains cognitive energy.

Constant worrying about body and food only harms mental health. If someone comments on your hair at a salon, just get up and leave.

The reality is,

  1. Movies create stereotypes.
  2. Social media creates comparison.
  3. Influencers create pressure.

All this leads to insecurity. That insecurity turns into shame.

This shame does not make people act. It makes them withdraw.

Even if someone changes, that internal shame often stays. So body shaming is never good.

If we understand what people go through internally, we can at least avoid doing it. Limit consuming fear-based and condescending content. It only increases insecurity.

Live a balanced, active life. Eat, live, and move on.

Unless it is your profession, overthinking body image and food doesn’t make sense.

Don’t stress too much about your body, your weight, or your hair.

That’s it.
